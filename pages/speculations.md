As noted in our [Methodology](/methodology), we attempt to make our speculations (literally) transparent throughout the project by rendering those elements less opaque than those about which we have more evidence. This page explains the speculations that we applied consistently across the village with minimal intentional exception to produce the credible variation that would be expected in a village.

Envisioning and modeling Seneca Village—a community that existed more than 150 years ago and of which there are no known surviving photographs or drawings—required imagination and informed speculation. All of our sources can be found in our [References](/references) list, and we give our thanks to our many advisors listed on the [About](/about) page.

## Village-wide

**Atmosphere**: Many details of the village environment would have differed day-to-day or season-to-season. We have represented Seneca Village on a late spring day in 1855, as it might have appeared when the sun came out after a rainstorm so as to render the streams and foliage more full and visible. The model’s background sky is cropped from Thomas Cole's 1836 oil painting *[The Oxbow](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/10497)*, representative of the work of the nineteenth-century Hudson River School of painters.

**Level of detail in the interactive model**: Considering our task to create an accurate model of a village that was home to more than 220 people and contained dozens of structures, yards, sheds, barns, stables, fences, fields, the number of details we could include and refinements we could attempt to imagine are endless. For the online model, we must also balance the number of details with the constraints of accessible, web-based interaction and bandwidth. We limited the interactive visualization to the resolution one might see at a distance, to maintain reasonable loading times within a web browser and to prevent excessive speculation. For instance, the model does not show doorknobs, house gutters, species of individual plants, nor three-dimensional crops and animals.

**Tour route**: While visitors within the interactive model are free to move through the village from any angle and choose their own route, the interface can be difficult to manage. We made navigation easier  by adding annotations that provide a set tour. The annotations take the visitor on a roughly clockwise route, beginning from the southwest corner of the village, and mostly show the village from approximately standing eye level, assuming visitors might like to imagine strolling through the village. Our [Tour through the Visualization](/tour) and [Map-Based Tour](/pdf/ESV.pdf) follow the same route.

## Buildings

**Building sizes** were determined by the dimensions of their footprints and number of stories (floors) indicated on the 1856 “Condemnation Map” made by Gardner Sage to assess properties to be seized for the construction of Central Park. Within the project, the distances between buildings and the footprint of each building are as accurate (to the inch) as the measurements given on the original map. Archaeological excavations in limited areas of the village have found the 1856 Sage map to be very accurate in each of these dimensions. The map indicates two types of houses in the village: frame houses of 1, 1½, 2, 2½ , or 3 stories and “shanties.” Floor-to-floor heights in frame houses are modeled at 8.5 feet for the ground floor and up to 7.5  feet for higher levels. Shanties’ interiors are modeled with minimum heights of 7 feet (at their shortest, beneath a sloped roof). In the nineteenth century, the term “shanty” was often used in a derogatory sense to malign the small homes of working-class people who often built them expediently and informally. The “shanties” in Seneca Village were all valued significantly higher by the 1855 New York State census taker than shanties in other areas of the city, suggesting that Sage might have used the term simply to indicate one-story, one-room homes.

**Rooflines**: Double-sided pitched roofs were assumed for all of the structures labeled as “frame” houses on the Sage map and for shanties the census taker valued at more than $500. Single-sided pitched roofs were assumed for lower-valued shanties, stables, outhouses, and some barns. For rectangular houses, we have assumed that roof ridges are parallel to the longest side of the house. We based these assumptions on photos of comparable historic structures in the region and on the idea that residents would economize, when possible, in building. However, many village houses had nearly square footprints, and thus we cannot infer the direction of a roof ridge from the footprint alone. In such cases, we rendered the roof ridge parallel to the street. This assumption stems from  practical concerns in the construction of side-by-side buildings, given the proximity of some neighboring houses in the village as well as the imagined potential for new building construction as the community grew.

**Building materials**: The 2011 archaeological excavations in the village (Wall et al. 2018) uncovered the foundation of the house of the Wilson family, which was composed of roughly cut pieces of Manhattan schist held together by mortar and sitting atop shallow bedrock about two feet below the original village ground surface. Manhattan schist is the naturally occurring bedrock in the area that breaks through the surface of the ground in many outcrops within the village. The Wilson house foundation is the inspiration for how all of the frame house foundations in our project are rendered. We assume these house foundations were dug a few feet deep, they rose between 6 and 12 inches above ground level, and wood-frame house structures were built atop them.

Wood-frame shanties are modeled with floorboards lifted between 6 and 12 inches from the ground, supported at their corners and (depending on their overall size) middle with stone blocks. Self-built vernacular architecture in nineteenth-century New York existed on a spectrum, and many structures started as single-room wood-frame shanties that, over time, were built out into larger, more permanent structures. Mindful of this gradual process of upgrading and the surviving letters of villagers asserting the City undervalued their properties when it seized them through eminent domain, about half the homes labeled “shanty” on the condemnation map are modeled with schist foundations.

**Wood siding**: The 1855 Sage map indicates all homes in the village were “frame,” in other words, built of wood. We thus depict wood siding on all building exteriors. We render boards of 6 and 9 inches wide and assume most homes were built with exterior boards running horizontally (clapboard siding), rather than vertically (board-and-batten siding) that became popular later in the century for domestic structures. All Angels’ Church was rendered with board-and-batten siding, because of surviving photographs of the church, after it was moved from the village, showing that type of siding. Similarly, AME Zion Church is depicted with board-and-batten siding based on surviving photographs of comparable church buildings in the Northeast.

**Age of homes**: We considered the approximate dates of construction of each of the village homes, based on tax, census, and other records, and depicted a greater level of weathering on the siding of older homes than on younger homes.

**Chimneys** are modeled based on building type. We speculate that houses had brick chimneys  based on archaeological evidence that  the Wilson house had a chimney of red, locally made bricks. Fragments of red brick were also found in the yard between the Webster and Philips homes. Thus, we have assumed many houses in the village had red brick chimneys. Based on comparisons with historical photographs of homes in the region at the time, we depict chimneys with dimensions of 1 to 2 square feet in plan, broader at their bases than tops, and extending 2 to 4 feet above the roofline. Chimneys are shown without stone caps because chimneys connected to cooking fireplaces or cookstoves were unlikely to have caps as they were in constant use. We have depicted the village’s shanties with smaller, metal stovepipes cut through the roofs and extending above the roofline, based on historical drawings and photos and the premise that they were more affordable and more mobile than brick masonry.

**Roofing materials**: Archaeological excavation uncovered evidence that the Wilson house had tin-coated iron roof tiles. We speculate that many houses had this same type of roofing that was more waterproof and fireproof than less expensive wooden roofing and more affordable than slate roofing. Slate is depicted on the roofs of houses with the highest value.

**Doors**: Where primary entrances are unclear from the sources, doors were placed facing roads. The Viele 1855 and Sage 1856 maps show the vast majority of the homes were oriented parallel to property edges along roads, suggesting residents were attentive to the relationships between their homes and nearby roads.

**Windows**: We assume that homes had larger windows than stables and barns, and we rendered all homes with double-hung windows, with each window composed of smaller panes (between 6 and 9 inches square). These assumptions were based upon the typical construction of windows in the Northeast during the period and the relatively high cost of large panes of glass. Preference was given to the southern exposure, for reasons of sunlight and warmth.

**Shutters**: We assumed that most homeowners who could afford them would have had shutters to help insulate homes in all seasons, reducing heating fuel needs in the winter and keeping houses cooler in the summer, and to protect valuable glass windows during storms. That said, most shutters could be detached and stored during the months when they were not needed. Roughly half of the windows are shown with shutters, and the other half without. More expensive houses are shown with painted shutters, made from wood boards fitted together with dovetail joints. Lower valued houses are shown with unpainted shutters (or ones with peeling paint) fitted together with nails and a wooden crossbeam.

## Landscape

**Trees**: Locations and sizes of trees are based on the “Topographical Map of the Lands Taken for Central Park” made by Egbert Viele in 1855, which depicts trees of four distinct shapes. We assume they indicate the following four general types of trees, and we have rendered them as such in our project:

1. The most numerous is a short, bushy tree shaped as a fruit tree or other small deciduous tree;
2. A large, bushy tree, shaped as a full-grown oak;
3. A large, rounded tree, shaped as a giant willow, found only along Seventh Avenue;
4. The least common is a tall, skinny tree shaped as an evergreen juniper or pine.

We have not assumed species, but have assumed shapes and replicated each instance of the four types included in the Viele map, totalling about 150 trees across the village. A few historical records indicate the presence of fruit trees in the village, while archaeologically recovered botanical remains suggest the presence of elder trees, among others.

**The Croton Receiving Reservoir** looms large in the village landscape. Its exact height above Seneca Village is unclear from primary sources, but its approximate height can be inferred. The Reservoir sat higher than the Distributing Reservoir at 42nd Street, which was situated 70 feet above sea level (or 120 feet to maximum water level), with 50-foot retaining walls assumed from archival photos. Therefore, the retaining wall for the reservoir at Seneca Village is shown with retaining walls at least 130 feet above sea level. One historic drawing of the reservoir shows land sloping up near the top of the reservoir wall on the northeastern portion of the village. Construction plans also depict the reservoir at about 4 feet above street level in the north and to up to 40 feet in the south. We also included a small doorway on the southwestern side of the reservoir wall, as depicted in that construction plan. The appearance of the wall in our project was inspired by a remaining portion still visible in the park today.

**Elevation** is based on the 1855 Viele Map that records measurements above sea level for the entire park at intervals of 100 feet. Contemporary maps and the 1934–1935 Department of Parks topological survey also show the current topography in greater resolution. The elevation model that forms the ground plane of our 3D model as well as the images in our tours synthesizes these contemporary and historic sources to produce a detailed estimate of the village’s topography before demolition.

**Water supply**: Residences lacked access to the Croton Reservoir by internal plumbing, although they may have been able to draw water from it along 86th Street, where it was close to street level. Primary sources also indicate a well adjacent to William Mathew’s house and two natural springs, one later known as Tanner’s Spring. We rendered Mathew’s well and Tanner’s Spring in our project with dirt paths to and from the spring, assuming it was a heavily trafficked route given the many people who would have gone daily to collect water. We also assume some villagers collected rainwater in barrels adjacent to their homes and thus depicted some.

**Streams**: The 1855 Viele map indicates a stream running south away from the spring at 84th Street at the eastern edge of the village, as well as one running west from Tanner’s Spring. We assume the flow of these streams increased seasonally and rendered gullies along their paths, depressed 1 to 2 feet below ground and surrounded by vegetation due to wetter growing conditions near these water sources.

## Human Elements

Based on our own instincts as well as recommendations from our advisory group, our project does not depict people. That said, Seneca Village was far from empty, and our visualizations attempt to represent that by showing various material traces of human activity. We aim to indicate how the everyday activities of villagers would have influenced the appearance of the village, including dirt paths, laundry lines, smoke from kitchen chimneys, and signs of building wear and tear from use over time. Children’s toys and games (e.g., hopscotch courts and balls outside the schoolhouse) and the tools for a few professions (e.g., cartmen and a possible blacksmith) are included. Spaces of work and/or congregation, such as churches and schools, or other areas with greater foot traffic are reflected on the ground. A well, barrels, and buckets indicate the common and necessary activity of gathering water. And animals and gardens, along with  fencing, speak to both sustenance activities and work.

**Desire lines**: We assume villagers would have regularly taken the shortest (or easiest) route possible between places within the village. Desire lines (paths worn by repeated use) are modeled, extending 15 to 20 feet in front of most doorways. Dirt areas in places with significant foot traffic are represented, such as the area next to Ishmael Allen’s shop for outdoor work and behind the school house where children might have played games like hopscotch. We also depict the Wilsons’ property with a cleanly swept dirt yard. Limited archaeological testing on the east side of the Wilson family’s yard suggests that they kept at least that portion of their yard devoid of vegetation, likely by regularly sweeping it. Yard sweeping is a common practice across the African diaspora and in many other cultures because of the benefits that it offers, such as discouraging rodents and insects from making their homes near human homes and animals from foraging too close to domestic areas.

**Cemeteries** are rendered with a mixture of grave markers. Most are informal stones (of slate or schist) or wooden (as boards or crosses) based on historical records, photos, and archaeological investigations of cemeteries specific to each of the three denominations present in the village as well as other African American cemeteries including the earlier Manhattan African Burial Ground.  The Rossville AME Zion cemetery in Sandy Ground on Staten Island and Mount Zion AME cemetery in Gloucester, NJ inspired our depiction of AME Zion’s cemetery in Seneca Village. Historic photographs of All Angels’ Church’s sponsoring parish, St. Michael’s of Bloomingdale, informed our rendering of All Angels’ cemetery. The number of burials in each church cemetery was based on New York City death records (that indicate the church that buried each deceased individual), parish burial records (in the case of All Angels’), and studies of historic cemeteries, especially [Elizabeth Meade’s 2020 dissertation](https://www.cemeteriesofnyc.com/map).

**Cultivated fields**: The appearance of these areas in our project gesture towards known agricultural activity in the village. Their size and location are drawn from Viele’s 1855 topographical map. In areas of the village with steeper topography, crop rows are rendered perpendicular to the slope to prevent erosion. The kinds of vegetables grown are not yet rendered with specificity.

**Fences** are depicted across the village  to keep animals penned, protect crops from animals, and loosely demarcate the sacred ground of cemeteries. We assume that fences were not used to mark property boundaries. Thus, we have rendered fences around cultivated plots and around properties that include stables or animal barns. Fences vary in height according to the type of animals they would have retained. Fences show openings for gates in the part of each cultivated field adjacent to the nearest house. Historical photos and studies of historic fencing inform the different types of fences we depict (in terms of construction methods).

**Barns, Stables, and Sheds**: The size and location of barns, stables, and sheds are based on the 1855 Viele and 1856 Sage maps. Their appearances are modeled from historic photographs and studies of historic farm architecture.

**Animals**: The archaeological record hints at the kinds of animals that residents would have owned. The 2011 excavations revealed skeletal remains of sheep/goat, pig, and cow, including all elements from head to hoof, suggesting animals were raised and butchered within the village. A few goose-sized bird bones were recovered, and although chicken bones were not, we assume some villagers kept them, and any remains would have been eaten by villagers’ dogs. In selected locations, we show pigs and chickens roaming around, as well as cows and horses (evidenced by the stables on the Sage 1856 map, a curry comb discovered in the remains of the Wilson’s home, and occupations of villagers that would have necessitated them) confined to wooden pens. We depict goats on the hill in the village that was known as “Nanny Goat Hill.”

**Tools**: The 1850 Federal Census and the 1855 NY State Census indicate villager’s occupations, while archaeological remains hint at other kinds of work that villagers performed. We rendered some of the tools related to such work in our visualization. For example, we depict a cart outside the homes of Andrew Williams (a cartman)and John Geary (the reservoir keeper), and other villagers known to have worked with horses.
