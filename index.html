Envisioning Seneca Village is an ongoing, collaborative digital project that imagines and depicts what this significant nineteenth-century village might have looked like in the spring of 1855, about two years before it was destroyed by the City of New York to build Central Park. The project is anchored in extensive scholarship and aims to make the village’s history of Seneca Village visible to a wide audience. It features an interactive 3D model (see below), a non-interactive Tour through the Visualization, a printable PDF Map-based Tour, and supplementary materials. We are working on updates and new features, so please visit our site again soon.

Seneca Village was a community established by African Americans in 1825, two years before emancipation was completed in New York State. By the mid-nineteenth century, the village was the home of at least 50 families—about ⅔ Black and ⅓ white, mostly Irish. It was situated on an approximately six-block area east of present-day Central Park West roughly between 82nd and 88th Streets, which was then a few miles north of the city’s urban core and between the villages of Bloomingdale to the west and Yorkville to the east. This location offered a safer, freer environment to live than downtown. Villagers built a thriving, complex, and heterogeneous community with three churches and a school. Seneca Village’s destruction by the city in 1857 abruptly ended three decades of successful efforts to nurture families, sustain livelihoods, and create community. It was also an early instance in a long history of American cities’ abuse of eminent domain to sacrifice Black neighborhoods for urban development projects—in this case, to build a world-famous park.

As residents scattered to different parts of New York and the Northeast, the park construction crew buried building debris, covering it with landscaped lawns, walking paths and the loop road. Without any visible above-ground features of the village, the community was nearly forgotten for almost a century and a half. Yet the lives the villagers made there left an archival paper trail and extensive material traces underground. Since the 1990s historians, archeologists, educators, descendants, and artists have mined the fragmentary sources, excavated the site, and sought new ways to recover the village’s history.

Despite all of the information that researchers have uncovered and pieced together, there is a lot that is still unknown about Seneca Village and the people who made it their home. It is hard to imagine what it was like to live there. No known photos or drawings of the village survive, and the present-day landscape of Central Park and of the broader surrounding area (now the Upper West and East Sides of Manhattan) is very different today from what it was like in the middle of the nineteenth century. Envisioning Seneca Village addresses these blindspots by creating visual interpretations of the village that integrate historical and archeological evidence into digital cartographic and architectural reconstruction. Through this project we seek to amplify existing scholarship, help visitors learn more about the village’s history, catalyze new research with the questions our visual interpretations raise, and above all keep the memory and spirit of this past community alive in the present.

Gergely Baics, Meredith B. Linn, Leah Meisterlin, and Myles Zhang are the co-creators of Envisioning Seneca Village. We integrate our expertise in archaeology, social history, historical geographic information systems (GIS), and digital architectural reconstruction. The project has been aided by research assistants, additional support from the Central Park Conservancy, and generous guidance from a diverse group of Seneca Village advisors and stakeholders.
